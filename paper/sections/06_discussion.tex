\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Deployment scope.}
\silr{} targets the medium-horizon advisory dispatch loop ($5$--$15$\,min cycles), not the sub-second AGC tick: LLM latency ($\sim$22\,s/step) precludes real-time control, and shadow execution corresponds to the power-flow study preceding each dispatch, with protective relays independent of the LLM layer.

\paragraph{Support-monotonicity.}
The admission invariant (Prop.~\ref{prop:inv}) assumes recovery needs no transient redistribution: $\psitwo$ forbids steps that overload a new branch, a condition verified empirically in both benchmarks (\S\ref{sec:eval}).

\paragraph{Scope and design properties.}
The security claim is scoped to a \emph{fixed} compromised-LLM adversary on a trusted simulator: a corrupted model behind an intact control stack (\S\ref{sec:threat}).

\paragraph{From gate to training signal.}
The process-reward results of \S\ref{sec:reward} close the loop on the admission story: the product order is not only the criterion a runtime gate should enforce but the unit of structure a policy can internalize. The same scalarization that is unsound as an admission rule (Prop.~\ref{prop:gap}) collapses the advantage signal as a reward (Prop.~\ref{prop:collapse}): whether projected into an admission score or a training signal, what the scalar discards is the same violation geometry, and preserving it is what defeats both failure modes.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}
Future work includes scaling the training-seed budget for the full-scale multi-family experiments and comparing against a learned process reward model with continuous output.
